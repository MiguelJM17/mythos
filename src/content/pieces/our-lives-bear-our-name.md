In this paper, I argue that whether or not our actions are determined, we must preserve moral responsibility to protect the meaning of human relationships and the foundation of justice; I am not claiming that our decisions lack causes. Rather, even if our decisions have causes, moral responsibility must exist, because our actions still express an author who listens to multiple reasons before making a decision. First, drawing on P. F. Strawson, I claim that gratitude and reproach answer to a person's intentions, which sustain the meaning of human relationships. Second, I argue that a just society must distinguish what a person does by choice from what they do without choosing in order to praise and blame justly, a distinction lost without authorship.

Hard determinists disagree. They hold that every action follows necessarily from prior causes, so no one is morally responsible, and society must rethink punishment and praise. First, they claim that interpersonal life does not depend on moral responsibility, since gratitude survives without it and resentment is better left behind. Second, they argue that a just society can protect itself without blame or merit, responding to wrongdoing through prevention and rehabilitation. Both sides recognize that actions have causes, yet they disagree on whether those causes leave room for a person to be the author of what they do.

Defenders of moral responsibility emphasize that relationships depend not only on what others do, but on the will their actions express. Strawson calls our responses to that will "reactive attitudes," stressing "the importance that we attach to the attitudes and intentions towards us of other human beings" (Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment"). We may be glad of a sunny day, yet we are grateful only to a friend who chooses to help us, just as we resent only a friend who chooses to betray us. Even if every choice has prior causes, a person's actions still express an intention toward others, while the rain expresses no concern or malice; having causes does not turn a person into the weather. Strawson holds that these attitudes are too deeply rooted in human relationships to be given up, since abandoning them would mean regarding each person as something "to be managed or handled or cured or trained" (Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment"). Relationships, therefore, depend on recognizing that our intentions are real: gratitude and reproach derive their meaning not from an uncaused free will, but from the choices we make toward one another.

Hard determinists accept that intentions are real but deny that this requires moral responsibility: drawing on Derk Pereboom's hard incompatibilism, they argue that intentions do not make anyone deserve blame. Nor does rejecting desert force us into the objective attitude, since we need to give up only the reactive attitudes that presuppose it. Gratitude survives, for "one can be thankful to a young child for some kindness without believing that she is morally responsible for it" (Pereboom, *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*), and so does reproach: we can confront a friend who betrays us, still answering to his intention, and ask him to change without believing he deserves to suffer. What disappears is resentment, and its loss promises better relationships by releasing us from destructive anger (Pereboom, *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*). That an attitude is deeply rooted, moreover, does not prove that it is justified. Society, however, requires its own argument, which Pereboom draws from public health: we quarantine a patient with a dangerous disease not because he deserves it but because others need protection, and only for as long as the danger lasts; in the same way, institutions can restrain dangerous offenders and work to rehabilitate them without believing that anyone deserves to suffer (Pereboom, *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*).

Defenders of moral responsibility reply that the quarantine analogy erases the very line justice depends on: a patient did not choose his disease, while an offender chose his act. A just society must tell a voluntary action from an involuntary one, or it could never punish or honor anyone justly. Since every action has causes, what matters is how they operate: past causes culminate in the present person, who weighs reasons into a decision. Even if a person's reasoning is determined, a new reason changes their decision only because they understand and evaluate it, something their genes or environment cannot do on their own. And if, as determinists grant, intentions are real, then someone who lies after weighing reasons expresses their own intention, which is why we blame them but not someone who lies under hypnosis. What separates them is the space of deliberation, where reasons are heard and where, through our consciousness, we reach a decision that new reasons could still change. Even if that space is determined, it is enough for responsibility, because every excuse we accept, whether an accident, a threat, or hypnosis, works by showing that an act bypassed this space, never by showing that it had causes. As John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza argue, responsibility requires exactly this capacity to be guided by reasons (Fischer and Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control*). Quarantine, by contrast, responds to the danger a person poses rather than to what they have done, treating them as a hazard to be contained. Yet Immanuel Kant insists that human beings must be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as means (Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*). Only when society praises or blames people for what they have chosen does it treat them as authors, and therefore as ends.

Hard determinists reply that being guided by reasons does not establish true authorship. As Spinoza writes, "men are mistaken in thinking themselves free; their opinion is made up of consciousness of their own actions, and ignorance of the causes by which they are conditioned" (Spinoza, *Ethics*). The capacity to weigh reasons is no exception: if two people lie after weighing reasons, but one was raised to consider consequences and the other never was, luck decided the quality of their reasoning. Determinists can grant that retributive punishment seeks justice rather than revenge; the problem is that it claims a person deserves to suffer, and desert of that kind requires being the ultimate source of one's actions, which no one is. The offender did choose, unlike the patient, but that difference calls for a different response, not for desert: a patient needs treatment, the offender needs reasons, and rehabilitation that teaches him to weigh them addresses him as a person rather than a hazard. A rational society, whose end "is nothing else but peace and security of life" (Spinoza, *A Political Treatise*), therefore answers an offender's hostility as Spinoza's reasonable person does, rendering back "love, or kindness" for hatred (Spinoza, *Ethics*), since "hatred can never be good" (Spinoza, *Ethics*), and addresses the causes of behavior rather than delivering desert.

Defenders of moral responsibility need not deny that behavior has prior causes; they deny only that those causes erase authorship. To demand that a person be the ultimate, uncaused source of themselves is to ask for something no one could achieve, whether determinism is true or false, since no one can choose the self that does the choosing; the problem lies not in persons but in the requirement. Luck may shape how well we reason, but if it excused the one who was never taught to weigh consequences, we would have to look to his parents, then theirs, in an endless chain with no author and countless culprits. Determinists themselves refuse that chain: they rehabilitate not a man's ancestors but the man, addressing his capacity to weigh reasons rather than conditioning a passive mechanism. Their own remedy, then, quietly relies on the very capacity that makes a person an author. Justice asks only that each person answer for what they chose: owning the act, repairing the harm, and bearing a penalty proportioned to the deed rather than to the danger they pose. A penalty for the deed treats a person as the author of what they did, telling them that the act was theirs; a measure based on danger alone treats them as a risk, and could confine someone before they had done anything at all, merely because they might. The same holds for gratitude: we thank the firefighter who saves a child, not the parents who raised him, because the courage was his. Without desert, that thanks would be either empty or a mere incentive, and an incentive treats him as a means rather than an end. Nor is this a defense of resentment: to reproach a friend is to hold him to what he did while leaving him free to reason about it, and such a reproach remains fitting even when nothing can change, because the act still bears his name. Our causes may write the circumstances of our lives, but only we sign what we do with them.
